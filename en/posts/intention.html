Starting from Intention: Is Language a First-Class Citizen?

Published Aug 27, 2026 · ~12 min read

On the problem of representing “what to do next” in robot learning

Introduction

What exactly is robot learning about? A very direct answer is: learning a mapping from intention to action.

In the mainstream robot learning paradigm today, intention is usually represented by language — you give the robot an instruction, and it outputs a sequence of actions. This is also a major reason why VLA (Vision-Language-Action) models represented by RT-2 [1], OpenVLA [2], and π0/π0.5 [3, 4] have exploded in popularity: language is a natural, general-purpose, infinitely composable conditioning signal, and the internet is full of language–video data that can be used for alignment.

But stepping back, is language really suited to be the “first-class citizen” of intention? Not necessarily.

Consider a scenario: you throw a toy hard into the distance. If you have trained your dog well, it will immediately run over, pick up the toy, and bring it back to you. Behavioral research has long shown that dogs mainly judge “what to do next” by observing your movements, gaze, and body orientation [16]; they can learn some words, but that understanding is associative, far from “understanding language” [17]. No human gave the dog any instruction, yet the dog completes a full loop of “run over — pick up — hand it back.”

What this example is meant to show: intention existed before language. Intention is basically the plan of “what to do next” itself, and language is just one (quite recent) encoding of it.

So perhaps the first step of robot learning should be to clarify: what can represent intention? That is exactly the question this article wants to discuss. We will first give intention a mathematical position (Section 1), then unfold along a continuous spectrum — from language (Section 2), to images/videos (Section 3), to the contextual injection of demonstrations (Section 4) — and finally return to a deeper doubt in the conclusion.

Language (symbols, abstract, ambiguous) → Image/Video (pixels, concrete, unambiguous) → Demonstration / Context (in-context, directly injected)

There is only one thread running through this article: the value of intention lies in injecting “what to do next” information into the mapping from condition to action, thereby compressing the uncertainty of actions and making learning easier when data is scarce.

1. Why Intention Matters: A Mathematical Perspective

Whether today's VLAs or action models centered on world models, a default setting is to learn a conditional policy:

$$\pi(a \mid o, l)$$

That is, a mapping from condition (observation $o$ + language instruction $l$) to action $a$. This setup itself is not the problem — the real problem is: the condition is often not specific enough. When the same observation and instruction correspond to multiple equally reasonable actions, this “mapping” breaks down.

1.1 Multivalued Mapping: An Ill-Posed Problem

Mathematically, what $\pi(a \mid o, l)$ learns is not a function but a multivalued mapping (relation): given $c = (o, l)$, there may exist $a_1 \neq a_2$ such that $p(a_1 \mid c) > 0$ and $p(a_2 \mid c) > 0$. In other words, the solution is not unique — a classic ill-posed problem.

The standard way mathematics handles ill-posed problems is to introduce priors or additional information to constrain the solution space. Intention is exactly such “additional information”: it explicitly exposes the degree of freedom hidden in “non-unique solutions.”

1.2 Multimodal Distributions: Being Able to Express ≠ Being Able to Solve

Viewed from the perspective of distribution shape, the same problem becomes clearer: because the same condition corresponds to multiple equally reasonable actions, the conditional distribution $p(a \mid c)$ is naturally multimodal. A mainstream approach in current VLAs is to use diffusion or flow matching so that the policy can express this multimodal distribution — the model is no longer forced, as early regression-style policies were, to “average” multiple reasonable actions into an action that fits none of them well.

But can flow matching really solve the problem? Not necessarily. Multimodality only gives the model the ability to express multiple possibilities; it does not resolve the ambiguity for it. Under action chunk prediction, this flaw is amplified: the model predicts a chunk of future actions at once and executes it open-loop; if the chunk itself involves multivalued mapping, every step is a “branching choice.” And if one step is wrong, the rest of the chunk is built on the wrong state — compounding error accumulates over time, and the longer the action sequence, the less trustworthy it becomes.

Therefore, resolving a multimodal distribution into a unimodal one — making “what to do next” have exactly one clear answer at every step — seems to become a necessity. And that is precisely what intention does: it explicitly extracts the degree of freedom that makes the distribution multimodal, feeds it to the model as additional information, and lets the distribution “tighten” again.

1.3 An Information-Theoretic View: Conditional Entropy and Mutual Information

A more precise statement can be given with conditional entropy. $H(A \mid C)$ measures how much uncertainty remains in action $A$ given condition $C$. After introducing intention $I$, the conditional entropy can be decomposed:

$$H(A \mid C) = H(A \mid C, I) + I(A; I \mid C)$$

where $I(A; I \mid C)$ is the mutual information between $I$ and $A$ given $C$. The larger it is, the lower $H(A \mid C, I)$ drops, and the smaller the search space of action prediction becomes. Therefore, what intention modeling does mathematically is to maximize $I(A; I \mid C)$.

Why does this matter? The key is sample efficiency: a low-entropy unimodal conditional distribution can be fit more accurately with the same amount of data, and the model does not need to waste capacity encoding ambiguity — in a data-scarce setting, this is a direct learning-efficiency gain.

2. Extending Intention from Language: Hierarchical Policies

Although we challenged many shortcomings of language as intention above, one thing is undeniable: in a world mostly built by humans, language can probably still serve as an important intention representation. But language is far from perfect as intention, because the same sentence can be understood differently by different people: when I ask a robot to make me a nice lunch, I have only told it that I need a meal — not what to take from the fridge or what kind of meal to cook. Zooming in: when I ask a dual-arm robot to put a banana on a plate, the instruction does not tell it whether to use its left arm or its right arm. These problems mostly stem from the fact that although language has extremely high information density, it still cannot express enough information, and is often ambiguous — especially geometric information (where to place the hand, in which direction to apply force) and degree information (how much, how fast), which are almost entirely missing.

In the end, more detailed language descriptions can yield more detailed intention representations. A simple way is to write instructions as detailed as possible; a more systematic route is the hierarchical policy, which separates “what to do” from “how to do it.” π0.7 uses subtasks for high-level planning, decomposing an ambiguous intention into a sequence of unambiguous subtasks and thereby making the ambiguity explicit [5]. RT-H takes this one level further: between task and action, it first predicts language motion (e.g., “move the arm forward”) — a semi-linguistic, semi-action intermediate representation — and humans can directly intervene and correct the robot at this level [6]. Incidentally, MUTEX lets the same policy accept task specifications in different modalities — text, image goals, video demonstrations — with the modalities reinforcing each other [7]. This already hints that language is not the only entry point for intention.

The gains on this path are clear: semantic ambiguity is resolved, with the bonus of intervenability. But it has a ceiling — it stops at what language can express. For what action to take next, language often simply cannot say it clearly.

3. Extending Intention from Images/Videos: World Models

We have discussed how to resolve ambiguity and enhance intention from the language side. But for what action to take next, language is often hard-pressed to describe it clearly. So what would be a better way to describe it? In human society, when a mother teaches a child to use chopsticks, she usually does not teach with language — she demonstrates. A demonstration may be a better intention representation: it packs in the goal, the ordering, and all the geometric details language cannot express.

But when actually deploying robots, we cannot always have a human demonstrate for the robot. Fortunately, video generation models have developed rapidly in recent years, giving robots a natural weapon: since no human is there to demonstrate, let a video generation model generate how to move next. This is what today's world models do.

3.1 Directly Generating Videos

The most direct approach is to generate a complete video of the future time horizon, drawing out the entire plan. LingBot-VA is a representative of this line of work: video tokens and action tokens are interleaved into a causal sequence, and a dual-stream Transformer jointly models “how the world changes” and “how to move next”; the action branch acts like an inverse dynamics model, decoding actions from the imagined frames. During execution, real observations replace the video KV cache to prevent the imagination from drifting too far [8]. UniPi is an earlier and more radical exploration — directly reformulating the policy problem as text-conditioned video generation: first generating a “plan video” with a diffusion model, then extracting control actions from it [9].

3.2 Generating Future Image Frames

Of course, generating full videos is expensive: inference is slow, and temporal consistency must be maintained. A lighter-weight approach is to generate only sparse future keyframes (subgoal images), treating them as anchors for intention and letting the policy fill in the intervening actions itself. This line of work traces back to SuSIE: an image-editing model “edits” the current frame according to the instruction into a subgoal image a few seconds ahead, which is then handed to a language-free goal-conditioned policy to approach [13]. π0.7 follows the same idea — the prompt can hold up to three multi-view subgoal images (the main view constrains the object and scene, the wrist view constrains the gripper pose), generated on demand by a lightweight world model [5]. Subgoal images turn the problem into inverse dynamics: given the current frame and a near-future frame, infer the action sequence in between. Language spells out “what to do”; images supply the “to what degree” that language omits.

Act2Goal makes sparse-frame imagination more systematic: a goal-conditioned visual world model imagines, from the current observation, a sequence of intermediate visual states leading to the goal image, then splits the imagined frames into near-term dense frames (responsible for fine-grained closed-loop control) and distant sparse frames (anchoring global consistency) [11]. PIDM (Seer) states it most plainly: a visual foresight module predicts future frames, and the action head is a pure inverse dynamics model — task planning and action prediction are completely decoupled [12].

What these approaches share is putting the “goal” into a visual space isomorphic to the observations: the model no longer learns “language → action” across a semantic gap, but rather “current frame → imagined future frame → action,” which is actually closer to an inverse dynamics problem. The cost is the uncertainty of generation itself — hallucination, drift — which needs engineering measures such as closed-loop correction as a safety net. GR00T-Dreams even goes the other way, using video world models to mass-produce training data [10] — a different direction for the same weapon.

4. Extending Intention from Demonstrations: Direct Injection via In-Context Learning

The routes in the previous sections — whether finer language or generated images/videos — all enhance intention at inference time through test-time intention prediction. In-context learning, which has been gaining increasing attention recently, enhances intention in the condition itself: without constructing or generating anything, it puts a demonstration directly into the context and lets the model “learn by watching once.”

GEN-1.5 drags 3–12 second demonstrations directly into the context window and executes with zero gradients; Generalist calls this physical prompting [14]. They say no special design was made for ICL — no meta-learning, no auxiliary objectives — the capability emerged naturally from large-scale pretraining, just like GPT-3's one-shot. Even more interestingly, two independent demonstrations placed in the same context are automatically composed into one continuous skill, with the model filling in the missing transition actions itself. Skild's S1 is even more aggressive: a single human video as prompt, zero fine-tuning, executing ten-minute-scale multi-step tasks [15]. And it treats the demonstration as a goal specification rather than trajectory reproduction — the demonstrator cracks eggs messily, and S1 completes the same step with clean actions — showing that what it reads from the video is “what to achieve,” not how to copy each action.

These two efforts are cut from the same cloth: the carrier of intention shifts from training parameters to the inference context; skills live in the context window and disappear when the window is cleared. In the language of Section 1, the demonstration is that “additional information” $I$, except that it is injected directly at inference time — “maximizing mutual information” turns from a training objective into an operation performed in the context.

Conclusion: The Future of Intention Representation (Without Intention Enhancement)

We have discussed three routes for enhancing intention: finer language descriptions, generated images/videos, and demonstrations injected directly into the context. But looking at animal behavior, perhaps none of these is the final answer. In a sense, all three emerged against the backdrop of severely insufficient embodied-AI data while language models and video generation models had already become powerful — they are really borrowing a neighbor's capability for test-time scaling: hierarchical models lean on LLMs to decompose subtasks, world models lean on video generation to “imagine” the future, and in-context learning feeds demonstrations straight into a pretrained model's context. Once data becomes abundant, the train/test gap will gradually narrow, and end-to-end models will probably learn most capabilities directly, the way today's LLMs do (most LLMs are still trained with teacher forcing, yet that does not stop them from working well on real tasks) — making these borrowed-power enhancements much less necessary. So in the long run, embodied AI will likely take the same path: training end-to-end models directly on enough data, with long-horizon planning becoming a focus just as RL has for today's LLMs.
